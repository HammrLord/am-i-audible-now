Abstract

The functional organization of prosody within the Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ) codebooks of neural audio codecs remains an open question. Prior work relies on correlational probing, showing that pitch features can be decoded from frozen codebook embeddings. Decodability, however, does not imply causal necessity: a codebook may encode pitch information the decoder ignores, while another may be essential despite containing little probe-recoverable signal.

We address this gap through causal output-side ablation: for each codebook, we zero or mean-replace its discrete tokens, re-decode the waveform, and measure degradation in fundamental frequency F0 (CREPE), energy (frame-wise RMS), and intelligibility (STOI) against the unablated reconstruction. Experiments span 200 LibriSpeech test-clean utterances under clean, 15, 5, and 0 dB SNR with MUSAN noise across multiple neural audio codecs.

Across different architectures, we find a consistent two-tier causal structure: a codec-specific subset of codebooks is reconstruction-critical, while the remaining intelligibility-preserving codebooks exhibit a stable pitch-contribution hierarchy that persists under additive noise. These findings provide new evidence for how neural audio codecs causally organize prosodic information.

Approach

A probe trained on frozen embeddings tells us whether pitch can be read out of a codebook. It does not tell us whether the decoder uses that codebook when it reconstructs pitch. To answer the second question we intervene on the codes directly. For each of the eight codebooks we either set its codes to zero (zero-ablation) or replace them with that codebook's most frequent code (mean-ablation), decode, and compare the output against the unablated reconstruction of the same input. Mean-ablation serves as a gentler control, because zeroing a middle RVQ layer also disturbs the residual assumption of every layer after it.

All three metrics are computed on the output waveform, at each codec's native sample rate. At every noise level the reference is that level's own unablated reconstruction and not the clean original, so the measurement isolates which codebook the decoder depends on from the plain fact that noise degrades everything. We study Mimi, SpeechTokenizer, and EnCodec, the last of which has no distilled semantic codebook at all.

Necessity without Specificity

Removing any codebook lowers intelligibility, but on Mimi one codebook breaks it outright: masking codebook 1 drops STOI to about two-thirds of the lowest value any other codebook reaches. Its large pitch effect is therefore confounded with general collapse. Among the rest, codebooks 2, 3, and 4 sit at nearly identical STOI yet differ by more than a factor of 2.5 in pitch effect, which a pure reconstruction-damage account cannot explain.

Stability under Noise

Once the confounded codebook is set aside, the remaining seven produce a pitch ranking that holds across noise conditions, and holds more firmly than the unfiltered eight-codebook ranking does. Spearman correlation between the clean and noisy profiles stays at or above 0.96 on Mimi under zero-ablation, at 15, 5, and 0 dB SNR.

Zero- and mean-ablation do not always agree

The two ablation modes agree only partially even on clean speech (ρ ≈ 0.68 on Mimi) and drift further apart as noise increases. We attribute this to the harsher disruption that zero-ablation imposes on the residual chain. Each mode remains internally consistent across noise levels, so our conclusions rest on within-method agreement and not on agreement between methods.

Related Links

This study builds on a line of work that asks what neural audio codecs represent, and we are grateful to the authors whose results motivated it.

Probing neural audio codecs for distinctions among English nuclear tunes by Vigneaux and Cole, and "Is prosody encoded in the neural audio codec Mimi?" by Ballier et al. (Speech Prosody 2026), find pitch recoverable from all eight Mimi codebooks. Our ablations ask the complementary causal question.

Amnesic Probing by Elazar et al. sets out the general argument that probe accuracy does not establish that a model uses the probed information.

"Probing the Robustness Properties of Neural Speech Codecs" by Tseng and Harwath (Interspeech 2025) benchmarks codecs under noise and reverberation as black boxes, and "Bringing Interpretability to Neural Audio Codecs" by Sadok et al. (Interspeech 2025) maps RVQ layers to content, speaker, and pitch with an association model.

Our measurements rely on CREPE for pitch estimation, LibriSpeech for speech, and MUSAN for additive noise.
